Why this matters

The variable type decides what you're allowed to do with it. You can average sleep_hours, but the "average favorite class" is nonsense. In Part 3 you'll see that each kind of analysis needs certain variable types.

Part 2 · about 8 minutes

Where's the middle?

If someone asks "how much do students in this class sleep?", you need one number that sums up the whole group. There are three common ways to pick it.

Mean

Add every value, divide by how many there are. The "average."

(6 + 7 + 8) ÷ 3 = 7

Median

Line the values up in order and take the one in the middle.

5, 6, 7, 8, 12 → 7

Mode

The value that shows up most often. A group can have more than one.

6, 7, 7, 8 → 7

The big idea

The mean gets pulled toward extreme values. The median stays put. When data has a few very large or very small values, the median usually describes a "typical" person better.

The mode is the only one that works for categorical variables. In our survey, the most common favorite_class is History (9 of 40).

Quick check

A news story reports the "typical" household income in a town where a few billionaires live. Should it use the mean or the median?

Median. A handful of billionaires would drag the mean far above what most families earn. The median shows what the household in the middle actually makes.

Can you find the mean of plays_sport?

No. It's nominal (Yes/No). You can report the mode, or a count: 19 of 40 students play a sport.

Part 3 · about 10 minutes

Three questions, three tools

Each analysis answers a different kind of question. Pick the tool by asking: what am I trying to find out?

Descriptives
"What does this group look like?"

Descriptive statistics summarize one variable: its middle (mean, median), its spread, and its lowest and highest values. Standard deviation (SD) is roughly how far a typical student sits from the mean.

t-test
"Are these two groups really different?"

A t-test compares the means of two groups. In our data, athletes average about 0.8 more hours of sleep than non-athletes. Is that a real difference, or just luck?

The problem: luck always makes a gap

Split any 40 students into two random groups and their averages will never match exactly. Some gap always shows up by pure chance. So the question isn't "is there a gap?" It's "is this gap bigger than luck usually makes?"

The test: pretend the group labels mean nothing. Shuffle the labels randomly across the 40 students and measure the gap again. Do that hundreds of times. The pile of shuffled gaps shows what luck alone looks like. Then see where the real gap lands.

Likely a real difference

Fewer than 5 in 100 shuffles made a gap this big. Luck is a poor explanation. Statisticians write this as p < .05.

Could be chance

Luck makes gaps this big fairly often, so we can't rule it out. This doesn't prove the groups are identical. It means our data can't tell them apart.

So what's a p-value?

It's the share of "luck-only" worlds that produce a gap at least as big as the one you found. A p-value of .03 means about 3 in 100. The 5% cutoff (1 in 20) is a convention, not a law of nature.

A t-test gets nearly the same answer with a formula instead of shuffling. And remember: "likely real" tells you there is a difference, not why. Athletes might sleep more because of early practices, or for some other reason.

Regression
"Does one thing predict another?"

Regression fits a straight line through two numerical variables. The line's slope tells you how much Y changes, on average, for each 1-unit increase in X. Try to draw the best line yourself first.

Reading the result

The best line is quiz = 88.2 − 3.1 × screen_time. Each extra hour of daily screen time goes with about 3 fewer quiz points, on average.

R² = .32: screen time accounts for about a third of the differences in quiz scores. The rest comes from other things. The shuffle logic applies here too: p < .001, so the pattern is very unlikely to be luck. It's still a pattern, not proof that screens cause lower scores.

Part 4 · about 12 minutes

Your turn in jamovi

jamovi is free statistics software that runs in your browser. You'll run all three analyses on the same 40-student survey and see if your numbers match what you saw in Part 3.

Set up · 3 minutes
  1. Save the data file.

    If the button doesn't work, get class_survey.csv from your teacher.

  2. Open jamovi cloud at cloud.jamovi.org. Sign in or make a free account if it asks.
  3. Open the file. Click ☰ (top left) → Open → Browse, then pick class_survey.csv.
  4. Fix one variable type. Double-click the grade_level column header. Change Measure type to Nominal. jamovi saw 11 and 12 and assumed they were amounts. They're really group labels, just like you sorted in Part 1.
Task 1 · Descriptives
  1. Click the Analyses tab → Exploration → Descriptives.
  2. Move sleep_hours, screen_time, and quiz_score into the Variables box.
  3. Look at the results table on the right. For quiz_score, which is bigger: the mean or the median?
Check your numbers

quiz_score: mean 74.7, median 73, SD 8.0. sleep_hours: mean 7.0. screen_time: mean 4.3. The quiz mean is a bit above the median, so a few high scores pull it up.

Task 2 · t-test
  1. Analyses → T-Tests → Independent Samples T-Test.
  2. Move sleep_hours into Dependent Variables. Move plays_sport into Grouping Variable.
  3. Under Additional Statistics, check Mean difference and Descriptives.
  4. Find the p column. Is it under .05? Decide: likely a real difference, or could be chance?
  5. Then swap: replace sleep_hours with quiz_score and plays_sport with grade_level. What does p say now?
Check your numbers

Sleep by sport: the means are 7.44 (Yes) and 6.61 (No), t = −2.89, p = .006. Likely a real difference. (The minus sign only means jamovi subtracted in the order No − Yes.)

Quiz by grade: means 74.2 (11) and 75.1 (12), p = .717. Could be chance. Same as the shuffles showed in Part 3.

Task 3 · Regression
  1. Analyses → Regression → Linear Regression.
  2. Move quiz_score into Dependent Variable. Move screen_time into Covariates.
  3. In Model Fit Measures, find R². In Model Coefficients, find the Estimate for screen_time. That's the slope.
Check your numbers

R² = 0.32. Intercept 88.2. Slope for screen_time = −3.13, p < .001. Each extra hour of screen time goes with about 3 fewer quiz points.
