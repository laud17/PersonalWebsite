AI and Team-Based CI
ASU Next Education Workforce

AI works differently
when teams are the unit.

Most educators are using AI on their own, for lesson prep and content generation. This workshop looks at what changes when a team uses AI together to run continuous improvement cycles. The difference is not the tool. It is the structure around it.

The argument: The one-teacher/one-classroom model is an architecture problem, not a pedagogy problem. AI hits the same ceiling every technology has hit, not because tools fail, but because the grammar of schooling pulls everything into individual practice. Team-based staffing creates the conditions for AI to work as a shared intelligence tool, not just a personal assistant.

Self-Assessment: AI Use for Team-Based Continuous Improvement

This short assessment looks at where AI currently shows up in your team's work, and whether that use is individual or collective. It covers the full PDSA cycle: planning, implementation, evidence gathering, and decision-making. You get a pattern interpretation at the end, plus one concrete structural move.

The assessment runs as a short AI conversation in Playlab. It takes about 5 minutes. Have your team context in mind before you start.

Open Assessment in Playlab

Four Common Patterns

Most teams land in one of these four places. Use these to frame your table conversation after the assessment.

Individual Planner
AI helps one person prepare for the team. Lesson plans, agendas, and content get generated before the meeting. The team never touches the tool together.
Next move: bring one AI output into a team meeting and study it together rather than delivering it as finished work.
Collective Planner
The team uses AI together to plan instruction. Roles, routines, and lesson structures get built in shared sessions. AI use is collective but still limited to planning.
Next move: use AI in the Study phase to analyze student evidence, not just to prepare for instruction.
Evidence Studier
The team uses AI to help make sense of student work and observation data. AI gets used mid-cycle, not just at the planning stage. This is uncommon and worth building on.
Next move: connect AI use in Study back to your aim statement. Make sure the evidence you are analyzing ties to what you said you would measure.
Full Cycle User
The team uses AI across all four phases. AI supports planning, surfaces observations, helps analyze evidence, and informs Act decisions. This is the target state for team-based CI.
Next move: document what is working and share it. Your team's practice is worth spreading to others who are earlier in the cycle.
Scenario 1 of 2

A Team Running Its Own Instructional Cycle

A 3rd grade math team at a school using a team-based staffing model. The team includes a lead teacher, two associates, and a student teacher. They have shared planning time three times per week. Their problem is not curriculum. It is what happens during small group time.

P Plan
Problem of Practice
During small group math blocks, students work side by side but rarely talk to each other about the math. When a student gets stuck, they wait for an adult. Misconceptions do not surface until the lead teacher or associate circulates and catches them one at a time. Peer conversation is not happening, and we are not set up to make it happen.
Aim Statement
By the end of this 6-week cycle, 75 percent of students will demonstrate at least one instance of peer mathematical explanation per observed small group session, as documented in weekly team observation notes.
Change Idea
Introduce an explain-and-challenge routine at the start of each small group. One student explains their approach to a problem. One peer asks one clarifying question before the group moves on. The associate runs this routine so the lead teacher can observe and take notes.
Prediction
If the routine runs consistently, students who rarely speak in whole-group settings will engage more because the stakes are lower. We expect the first two weeks to feel slow. By week three we predict discourse quality will improve. We also predict the associate will need support with facilitation in the first week.
Measures
Outcome: percent of students demonstrating peer explanation per session. Process: percent of sessions where the routine was used with fidelity. Balancing: lesson pacing, since the routine should not consistently cut into core instructional time by more than five minutes.
D Do
What Was Implemented and When
The routine ran in three small group sessions per week across weeks one through three. In week one the lead teacher modeled it before the associate took over. By week two the associate launched it independently at the start of each session.
Observations of Student Learning
Week one: most explanations were procedural. Students described steps but not reasoning. The clarifying question step mostly produced silence. Week two: two students began pushing back on each other without being prompted. Week three: six of nine students used math vocabulary during explanations.
Data Collected
Week one: three of nine students demonstrated peer explanation (33 percent). Week two: five of nine (56 percent). Week three: seven of nine (78 percent). Routine fidelity: eight of nine sessions. Two sessions ran six minutes over the five-minute pacing threshold.
Surprises and Deviations
Two students who almost never participate in whole-group instruction engaged actively in the peer routine. This was not expected. One session was cut short because of a fire drill and was not counted.
S Study
Prediction vs. Reality
We predicted early friction and improvement by week three. Both happened. We did not predict that quieter students would engage more than in whole-group settings. We hit 78 percent peer explanation by week three, ahead of the 75 percent target set for week six.
What the Data Says About Student Learning
The increase in peer explanation matches an increase in math vocabulary in observation notes. But we are measuring frequency, not quality. We do not yet know if deeper reasoning is developing or if students are meeting the form of the routine without the substance.
What We Learned About the Learning Environment
Students need a structured lower-stakes entry point to academic discourse. Whole-group discussion was not creating it. The peer routine reduced performance anxiety for at least two students. This is a design problem, not a student problem.
Confidence Level
Moderate confidence in the frequency finding. Low confidence that we are capturing quality. We need a way to distinguish procedural explanation from conceptual explanation, and to check whether discourse quality is showing up in independent written work.
A Act
Decision
Adapt. The routine is working but we are not measuring the right things yet. We will keep the structure and add a two-level explanation rubric. Not ready to adopt or spread.
Changes for Next Cycle
Add a student self-assessment prompt after each explanation: was that a steps explanation or a reasons explanation? Collect one piece of written work per week. Keep the routine structure unchanged.
Next Cycle Hypothesis
If students self-assess explanation type and we collect weekly work samples, we predict the proportion of conceptual explanations will increase from roughly 20 percent to 40 percent by week three of the next cycle.
What the System Needs to Do
We need 30 minutes at the next all-team PL session to share what we are learning, not just what we are doing. We also need access to prior assessment data to check whether discourse gains are connecting to formal assessment results.
AI Coaching Responses
Plan Phase
The aim statement is specific and time-bound. The prediction is the strongest part of this plan. It names expected friction, a timeline for improvement, and a specific risk around the associate's facilitation. That is a real hypothesis, not just optimism. The gap is in the measures. You have an outcome measure and a process measure, but the balancing measure only tracks time. Think about whether there is a student experience measure worth watching, something that would tell you if the lower-stakes structure is actually reducing anxiety or just adding a new routine students are performing.
Do Phase
The surprise about quieter students is the most important data point in this section. Treat it as a finding, not an anecdote. The gap is that you do not yet know if what students are saying is mathematically substantive. You are counting explanations. You are not yet describing them. Before you move to Study, agree on what a good explanation looks like so you can distinguish it from a compliant one.
Study Phase
This team is doing something most teams skip: they are being honest about the limits of their own evidence. Naming the difference between frequency and quality is a real analytical move. The question to sit with: your theory assumes the routine produces discourse, and discourse produces learning. You have evidence for the first step. You do not yet have evidence for the second. That is not a failure. It is what the next cycle is for.
Act Phase
The adapt decision is well-grounded and appropriately cautious. The next cycle hypothesis is testable and directly addresses the evidence gap from Study. One thing to watch: the student self-assessment prompt is doing a lot of work in the next cycle. Test it with two students before rolling it out to make sure it is simple enough to use honestly, not just correctly.
Facilitator Note
The key moment in this scenario is the Study phase, where the team names what they do not know. Point this out to the room. Most teams skip this step or treat the end of a cycle as a verdict. This team treats it as the beginning of the next question. That is the habit improvement science is trying to build.
Scenario 2 of 2

A Coach Running a Cycle About Teaming

An instructional coach at a middle school is working with a newly formed educator team: a lead teacher, two associates, and a specialist. The team has the right people. What it does not have is functioning planning meetings. The coach uses PDSA not to improve a lesson, but to improve how the team works as an improvement unit.

P Plan
Problem of Practice
This team's weekly planning meetings do not produce instructional decisions. People show up, the lead teacher runs through what is coming, and everyone leaves with tasks but not shared agreements. There is no shared look at student evidence. Associates and the specialist are not shaping the plan. The meeting format is producing compliance, not collaboration.
Aim Statement
By the end of this 4-week cycle, at least 75 percent of planning meetings will open with a structured 10-minute student evidence review, and at least two team members other than the lead teacher will contribute an instructional decision per meeting, as logged by the coach.
Change Idea
Introduce an evidence protocol at the start of each planning meeting. The coach selects two or three student work samples before the meeting. The team spends 10 minutes describing what they see before any planning begins. The lead teacher does not speak first.
Prediction
If the protocol runs consistently, associates and the specialist will begin offering instructional suggestions because the conversation will start with shared evidence rather than the lead teacher's agenda. We predict early awkwardness, especially for the lead teacher. We expect the specialist to engage most readily because she has the clearest role in the evidence.
Measures
Outcome: number of instructional decisions per meeting contributed by team members other than the lead teacher. Process: percent of meetings that used the evidence protocol for the full 10 minutes. Balancing: lead teacher's sense of meeting effectiveness, tracked through a brief weekly check-in.
D Do
What Was Implemented and When
The evidence protocol ran at the start of four consecutive weekly planning meetings. The coach selected work samples in advance. The lead teacher was briefed the day before each meeting. The coach observed and logged each meeting but did not facilitate the protocol.
Observations of Team Functioning
Meeting one: the team described the work samples but defaulted to the lead teacher to interpret them. No one offered an instructional suggestion unprompted. Meeting two: the specialist named a pattern and suggested a grouping change. Meeting three: one associate asked a question that led to a four-minute conversation about differentiation. Meeting four: the team debated two approaches to a reteach.
Data Collected
Protocol fidelity: four of four meetings. Instructional decisions from non-lead members: meeting one zero, meeting two one, meeting three two, meeting four three. Lead teacher check-in scores: 6, 5, 7, 8 out of 10.
Surprises and Deviations
The specialist engaged faster than expected, which matched the prediction. The lead teacher's score dropped in week two, which was not expected. In a brief conversation she said she felt less like the expert in week two. The feeling shifted by week three as she saw the team's contributions improving the plan.
S Study
Prediction vs. Reality
We predicted early awkwardness and specialist engagement. Both happened. We did not predict the lead teacher's week two dip or that it would resolve on its own. Growth in non-lead contributions across four weeks was faster than expected.
What the Data Says About Team Functioning
The protocol changed who speaks and when, and that changed what gets decided. But we are measuring contributions, not quality of contributions. We do not yet know if the decisions made collaboratively are better than what the lead teacher would have made alone.
What We Learned About the Team System
The team had capacity the old meeting format was not activating. Associates and the specialist have useful knowledge about students that was not entering the instructional plan. The protocol created a structured opening for that knowledge. Without the opening, it does not get used. This is a design problem, not a people problem.
Confidence Level
Moderate confidence in the meeting functioning data. Low confidence that improved team decisions are reaching students. We need to connect what happens in planning meetings to what happens in instruction, and then to student outcomes.
A Act
Decision
Adopt the protocol structure and adapt the evidence selection process. Right now the coach is selecting work samples, which is not sustainable. In the next cycle the team will rotate who selects evidence. That shifts ownership and tests whether the protocol holds without the coach.
Changes for Next Cycle
Rotate evidence selection responsibility across all four team members on a weekly schedule. Add one brief reflection question at the end of each meeting: did the evidence we looked at change what we planned? Track yes or no over time.
Next Cycle Hypothesis
If team members rotate evidence selection, we predict the quality of evidence brought to meetings will vary but the sense of shared ownership will increase. We predict at least one meeting where the evidence selection itself becomes a learning moment.
What the System Needs to Do
The principal needs to know this is happening and why. If the coach is asking teams to practice distributed leadership in planning meetings, the school needs to name that as a value. Right now it is a quiet experiment that puts the lead teacher in an ambiguous position.
AI Coaching Responses
Plan Phase
The aim statement is strong and measurable. The change idea is focused and bounded. The gap is in the theory of improvement. The plan assumes that starting with evidence will shift who contributes. That may be true. But it is worth naming what you think is actually blocking non-lead contributions right now. Is it the meeting structure, the team's sense of role, or the lead teacher's habits? The answer changes what you watch for in Do.
Do Phase
The observation notes here are unusually detailed. The week-by-week picture of who spoke and when is exactly what you need for Study. The most important data point is the lead teacher's week two dip. Do not let that become a footnote. It is evidence that the change cost something for the person with the most to lose.
Study Phase
The framing that this is a design problem, not a people problem, is exactly right and worth saying out loud to the team. The question this coaching would push on: you have evidence that team contributions increased. You do not yet have evidence that those contributions are connected to student learning. Name it as such and build it into your Act decision.
Act Phase
Rotating evidence selection is a smart and appropriately challenging next move. The system support point is critical. If the principal does not know this is the direction, the lead teacher is being asked to share power in a system that has not yet sanctioned that. That is not fair to her and it will limit how far this goes.
Facilitator Note
After this scenario, ask the room: what is the same about this cycle and Scenario 1, and what is different? The same: the logic of the cycle, the structure of prediction and evidence. The different: the unit of analysis. In Scenario 1 the team is studying students. In Scenario 2 the coach is studying the team. Both are improvement cycles. That is the reframe you want the room to sit with.

Plan / Theory of Improvement

Define the instructional problem, your aim, and the specific change you are testing. Your prediction is your hypothesis. Be concrete before you start.

01Problem of Practice
Describe the specific instructional gap your team is addressing. Ground it in what students are experiencing, not in adult behavior.
02Aim Statement
Who, what, how much, by when. Focus on student learning. Make it measurable and time-bound.
03Change Idea
What specific change will your team test? Be precise about what will look different in the learning environment.
04Prediction
If this change runs as planned, what do you expect to happen for students and why? A good prediction names a mechanism, not just an outcome.
05Measures
Name an outcome measure tied to student learning, a process measure tracking implementation fidelity, and a balancing measure watching for unintended effects.
P Plan Summary▼

Do / Implementation and Observation

Document what you carried out and what you noticed. Capture surprises, since they are data too.

01What Was Implemented and When
Describe how the change was carried out. Which sessions, which groups, what it looked like in practice.
02What Did You Observe About Student Learning
What did students do, say, or produce? Focus on learning behavior, not on what adults did.
03Data Collected
Report your measures. Partial data is still useful. Name what you tracked and what you did not.
04Surprises and Deviations
What happened that you did not expect? Surprises in either direction are data. Name anything that deviated from the plan.
P + D Plan and Do Summary▼

Study / Analysis and Learning

Compare what you observed to what you predicted. Go beyond the numbers. What does the evidence reveal about how students learn in this context?

01Prediction vs. Reality
Go back to your prediction. What matched it? What contradicted it? Name the specific evidence for each.
02What the Data Says About Student Learning
Interpret your measures. What patterns do you see? What questions do the data raise that you cannot yet answer?
03What You Learned About the Learning Environment
What does this cycle reveal about the conditions that support or constrain learning in your context? Look past the change idea to what it exposed about the system.
04Confidence Level
How confident are you in what you have learned? What would make your evidence stronger? Be honest about what you do not yet know.
P + D + S Full Cycle Summary▼

Act / Decision and Next Cycle

Based on what you learned, decide what comes next. Every Act is a new Plan. Your next cycle hypothesis closes the loop.

01Adopt, Adapt, or Abandon
State your decision and ground it in the Study evidence. Connect it to what you learned about student learning, not just whether implementation went smoothly.
02What Will Change in the Next Cycle
If adapting, name the specific modifications and the reasoning behind each one.
03Next Cycle Hypothesis
Write a testable prediction for the next PDSA cycle. This becomes the Plan for the next cycle.
04Spread and Scale Readiness
Is this change ready to spread to other teams or contexts? What conditions would need to be true for it to work elsewhere?
05What the System Needs to Do
Name what you cannot solve at the team level. What does school or district leadership need to do to make the next cycle possible?
