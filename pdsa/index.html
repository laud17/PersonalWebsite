AI + Team CI
ASU Next Education Workforce

AI works differently when teams are the unit.

Most educators are using AI on their own, for lesson planning and content generation. This workshop looks at what becomes possible when AI supports the full continuous improvement cycle of an educator team. The difference is not the tool. It is the structure.

The core argument: The one-teacher/one-classroom model is an architecture problem. AI hits the same ceiling every prior technology hit, not because the tools are weak, but because schooling absorbs them into individual practice. Team-based staffing creates the structural conditions for AI to work as a collective intelligence tool, not just a personal assistant.

Scenario 1 Team Instructional Cycle 3rd Grade Math

Improving Mathematical Discourse in Small Groups

A three-teacher team at an elementary school noticed students were not talking to each other during small group math time. They designed a simple protocol and ran a PDSA cycle to test it. The cycle is below, with the AI coaching responses they received at each phase.

P Plan
Problem Statement
During small group math rotations, students answer direct questions from the teacher but rarely talk to each other. When asked to discuss a problem with a partner, most students give short answers and wait for teacher confirmation before moving on.
Theory of Improvement
Students do not know what productive mathematical discussion looks like. If we give them a concrete two-step routine, they will engage in longer peer exchanges without waiting for teacher direction.
Change Idea
Explain-and-Challenge routine: one student explains their thinking on a problem, the partner asks one clarifying question before responding. The teacher only intervenes after both steps have happened.
How We Will Measure It
Tally the number of student-to-student exchanges per rotation (minimum 30 seconds, no teacher prompt). Target: at least 3 per group per session in the first week.
D Do
What We Did
Ran the routine in all three classrooms across two weeks, 4 rotations per day. Teachers tallied exchanges on a shared sticky note system. One teacher also noted which students initiated and which waited.
What We Noticed During Implementation
The routine worked better in the second week than the first. Students needed the first three sessions to understand that they were not supposed to wait for the teacher. Two quieter students, who rarely spoke in whole group, used the routine consistently. One classroom had difficulty because group sizes were four instead of two, which made it unclear whose turn it was to explain.
What Got in the Way
Larger group sizes created confusion about turn order. Two teachers were not consistent about waiting before intervening, which undercut the routine in those groups.
S Study
What the Data Shows
Average exchanges per rotation went from 1.2 in week one to 3.8 in week two. Groups of two hit the target more consistently than groups of three or four. The two previously quiet students generated 6 and 8 exchanges respectively across the two weeks.
What Surprised Us
We expected the stronger math students to drive the routine. The data showed that quieter students used it more consistently. We think the structure gave them a turn that did not depend on confidence or speed.
What the Data Does Not Tell Us
We counted exchanges but not quality. We do not know if students were asking good questions or just completing the steps to satisfy the routine.
A Act
Decision
Adapt and continue. Keep the routine but add a two-level rubric that helps students distinguish procedural explanation from conceptual explanation. Standardize on pairs rather than larger groups.
Next Cycle Hypothesis
If students can identify whether their explanation is procedural or conceptual, the quality of peer exchanges will improve alongside frequency.
What We Are Sharing with the School
The basic routine, the tally system, and the finding about quieter students. We think other grade-level teams could run a similar cycle with their own subject area version of the routine.
AI Coaching Responses
Plan Phase
Theory check: The team has a clear hypothesis but is conflating two problems: students not knowing the routine, and the classroom structure not supporting student-to-student talk. Both may be true, but they call for different responses.

What to sharpen: The measure counts frequency but not quality. You will know if students exchanged. You will not know if the exchange helped them think. Consider adding one observation note per session about what an exchange actually sounded like.

Watch for in Do: Teacher wait time is load-bearing here. If teachers intervene before both steps complete, the routine loses its core function. Track that separately.
Study Phase
What stands out: The finding about quieter students is the most important result in this cycle. The routine gave them a structural turn that did not require competing for attention. Name that explicitly in the Act decision.

Evidence gap: The team correctly identified that they measured frequency, not quality. The next cycle needs a different measure. The two-level rubric mentioned in Act is on the right track, but it needs to be observable from a tally, not a judgment call in the moment.
Facilitator Note
This scenario shows a team that designed a reasonable change idea and collected usable data. The AI coaching pushes on two things most teams miss: the difference between measuring activity and measuring learning, and the need to name unexpected findings rather than explain them away. Ask the group: what would this team have missed if they only looked at the weekly averages?
Scenario 2 Coach Improvement Cycle Middle School

Improving How a Team Actually Collaborates

An instructional coach at a middle school was working with a 6th grade ELA team. Meetings produced plans, but the same two people drove every conversation. She turned the PDSA cycle not on a lesson, but on how the team functioned.

P Plan
Problem Statement
In team planning meetings, the lead teacher and one other educator consistently frame the work for the group. The remaining two educators respond and affirm but rarely initiate. Meetings end with plans, but the plans primarily reflect the thinking of two people.
Theory of Improvement
The problem is not participation willingness. It is format. When the lead teacher speaks first and frames the problem, it sets a direction that is difficult to revise in real time. If the team looks at student work together before anyone offers an interpretation, more voices will enter the conversation before positions are set.
Change Idea
Evidence protocol: before any planning conversation, the coach selects three student work samples (anonymous, mixed performance). The team spends 8 minutes describing what they notice, without interpretation or planning. The lead teacher does not speak for the first four minutes.
How We Will Measure It
Coach logs speaking turns per person per meeting. Baseline: two people account for about 75% of statements. Target: no single person exceeds 40% of statements by week 3.
D Do
What We Did
Ran the protocol for four consecutive weekly meetings. Coach selected work samples 24 hours in advance. Lead teacher was briefed and agreed to the constraint. Coach tracked speaking turns with a tally on a notepad, logging initiation versus response separately.
What We Noticed During Implementation
Weeks one and two showed clear improvement in distribution. By week two, the two quieter educators were initiating observations. In week three, the lead teacher showed visible discomfort and privately told the coach she felt less like an expert and more like a participant. Weeks three and four showed a regression as the lead teacher began anticipating observations and steering them.
What Got in the Way
The lead teacher's expertise is real and her contributions were high quality. The constraint felt artificial once the protocol became familiar. The coach also realized she was selecting work samples that subtly highlighted her own instructional priorities, which introduced a different kind of framing.
S Study
What the Data Shows
Week 1: Lead teacher 68%, others 10%, 12%, 10%. Week 2: Lead teacher 44%, others 22%, 18%, 16%. Week 3: Lead teacher 52%, regression toward prior pattern. Week 4: Similar to week 3. The protocol worked as a structural intervention but did not hold once the lead teacher adapted to it.
What Surprised Us
The lead teacher's private comment about feeling less expert was the most important data point. It suggests the problem was not that she dominated the conversation intentionally. Her sense of professional identity was organized around being the expert voice. The protocol challenged that, which she experienced as a loss, not a norm change.
What the Data Does Not Tell Us
We tracked speaking turns, not quality of thinking. We do not know if the observations made by the quieter educators were actually influencing planning decisions, or if they were acknowledged and then set aside.
A Act
Decision
Adopt the protocol structure but change who selects the work. Rotate evidence selection to a different team member each week. This removes the coach's framing from the process and gives each educator a different kind of responsibility in the meeting.
Next Cycle Hypothesis
When a non-lead teacher selects the evidence, their status in the conversation shifts before the meeting starts. That status shift will carry into how the group reads their observations during the protocol.
What Needs to Go to Leadership
The lead teacher's identity concern is a school culture issue, not a coaching issue. The principal needs to publicly name distributed expertise as the expectation, not something the team is experimenting with in isolation.
AI Coaching Responses
Plan Phase
Theory check: The coach has named format as the driver, which is more precise than "the lead teacher dominates." Good. But the theory assumes that if you remove first-mover advantage, participation will distribute. That is a reasonable hypothesis, but it is not tested. What if the quieter educators do not have an internalized model of what a useful observation sounds like? The protocol might create space they do not yet know how to fill.

Watch for in Do: Track initiation separately from response. A person who responds to every observation is not the same as a person who initiates one.
Study Phase
Most important finding: The lead teacher's private comment about feeling less expert is your most useful data point, and it is not in your quantitative measure. Name it explicitly in Act. It tells you the problem is structural and cultural, not just procedural.

What the team is not seeing: The coach introduced her own framing by selecting the samples. You cannot test whether the protocol distributes thinking if the evidence was pre-framed by the facilitator. The Act decision to rotate selection is correct, and it should have happened in week two, not week five.
Act Phase
Decision quality: Rotating evidence selection is a strong move. It redistributes expertise claims before the conversation begins, which is upstream of speaking turns.

For leadership: The coach is right that the lead teacher's identity concern requires principal sanction. Without it, the next cycle will face the same regression. This is a prerequisite, not optional coaching guidance.
Facilitator Note
This scenario is more complex than Scenario 1 because the improvement target is relational. Ask the group: what would a traditional professional development session have done with this team? What does the PDSA cycle let the coach do that a workshop could not? The key tension is between structural intervention and cultural change. The protocol worked structurally but ran into an identity wall. The AI pushes on that distinction directly.
Plan — Define the problem and the change idea

Describe what you are trying to improve, why you think the change will help, and how you will know if it worked.

P1Problem Statement
What specific problem are you trying to solve? Be concrete. Avoid general descriptions like "student engagement is low."
P2Theory of Improvement
If we do X, then Y will happen, because Z. State the logic, not just the action.
P3Change Idea
What will the team actually do differently? Describe it so that someone not in the room could replicate it.
P4How You Will Measure It
What data will you collect? Be specific about what counts as a positive result.
Do — Run the change and capture what happens

Document what you actually did, what you observed, and what got in the way. Capture it close to the action, not from memory.
